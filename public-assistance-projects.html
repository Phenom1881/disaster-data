Where the recovery dollars go.

When a disaster overwhelms a community, FEMA's Public Assistance program reimburses state, local, and tribal governments — for the emergency response, and for rebuilding the public infrastructure the storm took out. Here is where that money has gone.

By category

What the money paid for

Every PA project falls into one of eight damage categories — grouped into the immediate emergency work, the longer permanent work of rebuilding, and the management cost of running the grants.

Emergency workA–B · response & protection
ADebris Removal
$38.4B · 121K projects
Clearing and disposing of disaster debris — downed trees, wreckage, sediment — from public roads and property to make an area safe and passable.
BEmergency Protective Measures
$96.2B · 214K projects
Actions to save lives and protect property before, during, and right after an event — evacuations, sandbagging, sheltering, and the bulk of the 2020 pandemic response.
Permanent workC–G · rebuilding infrastructure
CRoads & Bridges
$24.6B · 96K projects
Repairing or replacing the roads, bridges, culverts, and traffic controls that move people and goods.
DWater Control Facilities
$9.1B · 18K projects
Restoring dams, levees, channels, and drainage works that hold water back or carry it away safely.
EBuildings & Equipment
$41.8B · 132K projects
Rebuilding public buildings — schools, hospitals, offices — and replacing the equipment and vehicles inside them.
FUtilities
$33.5B · 74K projects
Restoring public water, sewer, power, and communications systems.
GParks, Recreational & Other
$16.2B · 58K projects
Repairing parks, playgrounds, and other public facilities that don't fall under the categories above.
ManagementZ · administering the grants
ZManagement Costs
$23.8B · 75K projects
The administrative cost of running these grants — staff time and overhead to document, manage, and close out projects.

Method

About this data

Figures come from OpenFEMA's Public Assistance Funded Projects Details, every funded project with a declaration date in 2000 or later. "Federal share obligated" is the federal government's committed portion — not the full project cost, and not necessarily the amount finally spent. One disaster can fund tens of thousands of separate projects across many applicants.

Disaster Data · A community project. Public Assistance figures from OpenFEMA, refreshed weekly. Federal share obligated reflects committed funding and may be revised as projects close out. Not affiliated with FEMA or DHS.
